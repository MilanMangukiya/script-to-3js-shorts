# What if Earth hit 1000°F for 5 seconds?

- **Slug:** `earth-1000f-five-seconds`
- **Duration:** 56s
- **Core Question:** If the air everywhere hit 1000 °F for five seconds, would you survive, and what would those five seconds actually destroy?

## youtube_shorts
**Title:** What if Earth hit 1000°F for 5 seconds?

You're out jogging when the air hits a thousand degrees Fahrenheit, hotter than the surface of Venus. It lasts five seconds. In this 56-second 3D science thought experiment, you'd probably survive: air holds so little heat that only your skin warms up, and you walk away with a bad sunburn.

Thinner things aren't so lucky. Flying insects drop in the first second, leaves boil from the inside, and dry paper and dead grass catch fire. When the five seconds end, the fires keep burning, and by the next morning summer trees have turned autumn brown.

Where would you want to be for those five seconds?

Scope: Fictional premise: for exactly five seconds the air everywhere is 538 °C (1000 °F) at normal pressure, then it returns to normal; the ground, water, people and objects start at their usual temperatures. Skin, leaf, insect and paper temperatures are estimates from simple heat-transfer models. A healthy adult who shuts their eyes and holds their breath would most likely get a painful first-degree burn, possibly with some blistering; children, the elderly and anyone who breathes in the hot air would fare worse. Ignition of paper and dry grass within five seconds is likely but not certain. Water vapour boiled off the leaves stays invisible until the air cools. "Most of the world’s leaves" excludes plants under snow or water and thick succulents. Venus averages about 870 °F at the surface. The jogger, walkers and fires shown are illustrative.
