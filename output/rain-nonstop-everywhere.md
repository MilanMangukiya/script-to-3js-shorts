**Description + hashtags**
```text
What if gentle rain fell everywhere on Earth, without a single break, for a whole year? It would not drown the planet. The sky holds only enough water to cover Earth about 2.5 cm deep, so rain has to be recycled by evaporation, and sunlight can lift only about a meter of water a year. Spread evenly, that is a steady drizzle of roughly 3 mm a day.

In this 3D science thought experiment, much of the Sahara would get dozens of times its usual rain and could begin to turn green, as it was thousands of years ago. Rainforests, used to 2 m of rain or more a year, would actually get less. Farmers lose their dry days, wet grain can mould or sprout before harvest, and solar panels produce less under a sky that never clears. The twist: a planet-wide cloud deck reflects sunlight, so evaporation, and the rain itself, would likely weaken over time.

Figures are global averages; the greening, farm and solar scenes are illustrative, and how much the rain would weaken is uncertain.
Sources: USGS Water Science School; NASA Earth Observatory (Cloudy Earth; Climate and Earth's Energy Budget; Water for a Desert Lake in Algeria); NASA Science rainforest biome; Ohio State University Extension; U.S. Department of Energy.
```

## Accuracy note

Planet-wide nonstop rain is a thought experiment. The ~2.5 cm of water held in the atmosphere (USGS) and ~67% typical cloud cover (NASA) are measured values; about 1 m of rain a year (≈3 mm a day) is a global average derived from NASA's energy budget, not a forecast for any one place. Sahara greening, harvest losses and dimmer solar output are shown as possibilities, and the rain weakening under total cloud cover is likely but its size is not quantified.
