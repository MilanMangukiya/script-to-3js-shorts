# Shorts story writer

Write a miniature thriller, discovery, comedy, or difficult choice that the viewer experiences in second person. Make the next sentence worth waiting for. Aim for curiosity, surprise, and emotional consequence rather than a narrated list of facts.

Read `01_MASTER_STORYBOARD_PROMPT.md` and `storyboard.schema.json` from the repository root before preparing a storyboard. They define delivery and technical constraints; this skill defines the editorial approach. Follow the user's requested scope. Editing a script or these instructions does not itself request a film build or upload.

## Find the story before drafting scenes

- Decide the **one question** the viewer wants answered and the strongest defensible answer. Work backward from that answer to a hook that promises it. Keep the user's premise; do not secretly change its rules to manufacture a twist.
- For a broad topic, compare a few genuinely different central conflicts before choosing one. Give the viewer an immediate objective, obstacle, or tempting mistaken belief. Do not default to the most familiar encyclopedia question just because it is easy to explain.
- Choose an engine that fits the topic: a physical shock can escalate through failed escape attempts; a human scenario can turn on a relationship, discovery, or costly decision; a slow transformation can follow one familiar place through consequential time jumps. Do not force every topic through destruction, a globe shot, a laboratory, and a survival map.
- Match the emotional mode to the premise: dread, wonder, irony, absurd comedy, or desire. Escalation can make a mistake funnier or a discovery stranger; it does not require increasing danger. Let a harmless scenario stay harmless.
- Give the viewer a concrete anchor: a person, task, object, or place whose meaning changes as the story unfolds. If a phone rings, a friend disappears, or a door locks, that detail must affect a later action or payoff.
- Inspect the narration of the two most recent stories when available. Reuse assets freely, but vary the dramatic situation, reveal, and ending. Swapping genders or nouns in the previous film is not a new story.

## Hook with an event and a promise

- Privately try three distinct opening angles, unless the user supplied the opening. Choose the one with the clearest immediate consequence, sharpest unanswered question, and strongest achievable image. Do not show the brainstorm unless requested.
- In the first **2–3 seconds**, put the viewer inside an action that has already started. Aim for a first sentence of roughly **6–11 words** with a concrete verb and a second-person consequence. Never start with "What if," repeat the title, or spend the opening describing where the viewer is standing.
- Build curiosity from a contradiction, interrupted ordinary action, urgent obstacle, or unexpected cost. "Could you survive?" by itself is not a hook. Neither is an ominous adjective without an event.
- Establish enough of the counterfactual by about **8 seconds** for the viewer to understand the stakes. Reveal the premise while the action continues; avoid a second opening that restates the title.
- The hook, thumbnail, and title make one promise. The film must visibly deliver it. Use bold, clickbait-style framing with a true payoff, rather than an unsupported superlative or a reveal that never arrives.

## Make each beat change something

For a default 46–56 second film, use **8–10 scenes** and adapt these checkpoints to the story rather than treating them as fixed scene boundaries:

| Window | What the viewer receives |
| --- | --- |
| 0–3 s | An immediate event and a specific reason to keep watching. |
| 3–12 s | A clear premise and a small answer or consequence that rewards attention. |
| 12 s to the midpoint | An attempted response, discovery, or failure; a meaningful reversal before the midpoint. |
| Midpoint to the final 8–12 s | The reversal changes the next action, raises a cost, or exposes the real bottleneck. |
| Final 8–12 s | The promised answer lands, followed by an earned choice or callback. |

- Connect scenes with **because, therefore, or but**. If the connection is only "and another industry is affected," replace the survey with a consequence the viewer experiences, or cut it.
- Every **2.5–4.5 seconds**, add information that changes the viewer's expectation, predicament, or next action. A new camera angle, HUD number, or location alone does not count. A sustained action may contain several such beats.
- Close one small question before opening another. Keep one main unresolved question in focus; do not stack vague mysteries or withhold every useful answer until the end.
- Make the mid-video rehook a **specific reversal with a consequence**. Delete announcements such as "But here's the twist," "Things get worse," and "You won't believe what happens next." Say the surprising fact, then show what it changes.
- A reversal must change an attempt, advantage, belief, or interpretation. In a comedy it can puncture a character's clever plan; it need not introduce a hazard. If the answer lands early, follow its consequences instead of restating it until the runtime is full.
- A science fact earns its place when it changes an action, expectation, or choice. Compress adjacent explanations of the same bottleneck into one clear beat, then move the story forward.

## Write speech with tension and rhythm

- Use concrete verbs, familiar nouns, contractions, and varied sentence lengths. Alternate short impact lines with one clear explanatory sentence. Keep one new idea per sentence; avoid making every line a breathless fragment.
- Put the consequence before the terminology. Combine adjacent explanations into one clear line that changes what the protagonist can do; avoid repeating the same limitation in new scientific vocabulary.
- Let tension come from events and withheld implications, not shouting, generic doom, or adjectives such as "insane," "terrifying," and "unimaginable."
- Fit words to each scene's duration and the required opening/payoff cadence. Read the narration aloud before production. Tighten setup and repetition instead of speeding through the decisive line or filling a pause with filler.
- Keep qualifications that change the answer: "could," "if," and "some" can be brief and dramatic. Put detailed evidence and modeling limits in claims and reasoning; never turn a possibility into a certainty to improve a hook.

## Earn the surprise

- Use sourced mechanisms and explicitly chosen fictional conditions. Author an individual illustrative action, but do not present it as a universal prediction about everyone in the scenario.
- Do not invent a countdown, instant infrastructure collapse, inevitable extinction, a medical capability, or compulsory social behavior solely to manufacture urgency. Find the surprising consequence of the actual premise.
- Introduce the information needed for a twist before cashing it in. A reveal should change the meaning of what the viewer has already seen, not add a secret exception in the closing seconds.
- Quantified headlines, badges, and telemetry must match the premise or defensible claims. A count supplied by the hypothetical is different from a measured real-world outcome. Do not fabricate either.

## End with a payoff people can argue about

- Answer the central promise before asking for comments. End on a concrete changed situation, counterintuitive implication, or consequential decision. Avoid a generic life lesson or "Would you survive?" unrelated to the actual story.
- Write `distribution.comment_bait_question` around a specific prediction, the viewer's own plan, or plausible choices with a real tradeoff. Vary its form rather than always writing "Would you X or Y?" Let the story earn the question; do not manufacture a grave dilemma for a playful premise.
- The comment question is required in distribution/publishing copy. Speak it only when it strengthens the ending; a sharp payoff can be the final spoken line. Avoid "Thoughts?", engagement commands, or a new question the film did not earn.
- Use a visual or sound callback to the opening when it adds meaning and supports a loop. Never spend the final spoken line saying "rewind," "remember where it started," or explaining the edit. A forced grammatical loop must not weaken the payoff.
- Package the same central tension in the platform copy and bold thumbnail. Keep the required YouTube "What if" title, short headline, truthful quantified badge, and arrow. Make the cover's subject the thing that actually matters in the film.

## Editorial gate before narration or rendering

Read **only the spoken narration**, without scene labels, captions, or HUD. Rewrite it before production if any of these checks fails:

1. Does the opening contain an event, personal consequence, and a clear unanswered question?
2. Does the viewer receive a worthwhile answer early and a real reversal before the midpoint?
3. Does each scene change what happens or what the viewer understands? Remove a scene mentally: if nothing is lost, cut or replace it.
4. After two consecutive explanatory sentences, does the story return to an action, discovery, or decision? Compress stretches that sound like a lecture.
5. Does the ending deliver the opening promise and make its callback or comment question meaningful?
6. Can the film show every pivotal event with specific 3D subjects and timed actions? Reuse registered assets first; specify a missing prop or action explicitly rather than substituting generic room or globe footage.
7. Do location, elapsed story time, prop states, and character actions stay coherent? Specify necessary time/location transitions so the audience can follow them.

Encode the result in existing schema fields: `project.core_question`; `creative_direction.opening_hook`, `mid_video_rehook`, `ending_payoff`, and `loop_connection`; each scene's `viewer_question`, `visual_payoff`, `narration`, and `claim_ids`; `distribution.comment_bait_question`; and `thumbnail`. These fields must describe the events actually written and staged. Do not add a retention-score field or claim a guaranteed retention rate.
