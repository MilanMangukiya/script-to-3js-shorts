# What if the sky disappeared?

## youtube_shorts
**Title:** What if the sky disappeared?

You step outside at noon and the sky goes black. The sun is still blazing and you can still breathe: only the blue is gone. In this 53-second 3D science thought experiment, the air stays but stops scattering sunlight.

That blue was sunlight bouncing off the air, and it was lighting every shadow. Without it, shade turns nearly black, stepping into it feels like driving into a tunnel, and a pedestrian standing in it can vanish from a driver's view. Wait in the shade and bright stars come out at noon. Then the sun sets white, with no orange glow and no dusk, and the next morning it simply switches on.

Which would you miss first: blue sky, sunsets, or being able to see in the shade?

Scope: Fictional optical premise: at noon on a clear equinox day at about 30°N, air and haze stop scattering visible sunlight; breathing, air pressure, weather and clouds are unchanged, and a cloudless day is shown. The shade-light figure (about one fifth of normal) and the sun-to-shade contrast (about 45 times) are estimates for one 22 m street canyon, not universal values. The near miss is an illustrative authored event. Star visibility assumes deep shade and adapted eyes: bright stars, not a full night sky. Sunset and sunrise are time-compressed; at 30°N the disc takes about 2.5 minutes to set or rise, and without twilight ground light falls from sunlight to starlight in that time. Brightness is shown with eye-adaptation exposure. Longer-term climate and ecological effects are not modelled.
